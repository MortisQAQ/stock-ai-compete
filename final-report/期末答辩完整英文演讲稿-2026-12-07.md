## 01 · Introduction — Background and Motivation (~2:30)

### [Slide 1 · Title | ~0:15]

Good afternoon, professors. My name is Haonan Jiao, and today I am presenting my Final Year Project, **openOutcry**.

The idea is simple: instead of asking an AI investment agent for one convincing stock recommendation, we want a place where people can **test, observe, and improve** an agent across a sequence of investment decisions. Before showing the product, let me explain why this problem matters now.

### [Slide 2 · Financial AI becomes accessible | ~0:35]

Financial AI is moving beyond summarising reports. Commercial assistants can help with financial research and modelling, while tools such as iWenCai's SkillHub make financial analysis capabilities easier to connect to AI assistants. These products are valuable: they lower the cost of asking sophisticated financial questions.

But an assistant that produces a good analysis is not necessarily an agent that makes good decisions over time. Research assistance and continuous investment evaluation are different problems.

### [Slide 3 · Open-source momentum | ~0:30]

The open-source community shows similar interest. Projects such as TradingAgents demonstrate how multiple AI roles can collaborate on financial research. Online demonstrations also show that investors and developers are curious about AI trading agents.

However, popularity is evidence of **interest**, not evidence of investment performance. A large number of stars, views, or persuasive explanations cannot tell us whether an agent manages risk over several months.

### [Slide 4 · Two unanswered questions | ~0:40]

This leads to two questions.

First, if we place **our own agent** into a historical A-share market environment and let it make repeated decisions, can we clearly measure its returns, drawdowns, and behaviour under trading constraints?

Second, if we change its prompt, research tools, or risk settings and run it again, can we tell whether that change genuinely helped, rather than just observing a different market period?

I am not trying to claim that today's financial tools cannot answer any part of these questions. The challenge is connecting the pieces into a usable, inspectable workflow.

### [Slide 5 · Roadmap | ~0:30]

I will present five parts. First, this background. Second, the users and their requirements. Third, the proposed solution and its system design. Fourth, what I have implemented so far, including a demonstration and an honest evaluation. Finally, I will summarise the contributions, limitations, and next steps.

Let us begin with the people who need this workflow.

## 02 · User Problems and Requirements (~2:30)

### [Slide · One convincing prediction is not validation | ~0:40]

Imagine an investor using an AI agent to analyse a stock. The agent gives a convincing explanation, recommends buying, and the price rises the following day. That is exciting. But is **one correct recommendation** enough to trust this agent?

What if the market falls? What happens after ten or fifty decisions? And what if the user changes one instruction and tries again? A polished report alone cannot answer those questions.

This is an illustrative scenario, not a claim that we conducted a user interview.

### [Slide · Two audiences | ~0:35]

There are two audiences for openOutcry.

The first is an investor who can use AI but does not want to build an entire simulation infrastructure. This person needs a lower barrier to experimentation and a clearer explanation of risk.

The second is an agent developer who modifies prompts, models and tools. This person needs a controlled way to compare behaviour before and after a change.

One group needs **accessibility**; the other needs **credible comparison**. Both need to see what the agent actually did.

### [Slide · The workflow gap | ~0:40]

Existing alternatives solve different parts of the task. Financial assistants are good at research; quantitative backtesting frameworks are good at structured strategy simulation; paper-trading platforms show portfolio activity; and public AI competitions can compare selected agents.

But a user bringing a custom agent may still have to connect market data, a decision loop, trading rules, event records and evaluation tools. The missing value is not necessarily another prediction model. It is a coherent workflow around the user's own agent.

### [Slide · Four requirements | ~0:35]

I therefore defined four requirements.

**Easy onboarding:** users should be able to start with a template or connect their agent.

**Realistic constraints:** decisions should be tested against a historical market clock and A-share trading rules.

**Understandable outcomes:** the user should see both portfolio results and the reasons behind important decisions.

**Repeatable comparison:** changing one configuration should lead to a new run under documented and, where possible, matched conditions.

These are product requirements, not claims that all four are already complete. Now I will show how the architecture is intended to satisfy them.

## 03 · Proposed Solution and System Design (~4:00)

### [Slide · The central proposition | ~0:40]

openOutcry is designed as an **evaluation and iteration environment for investment agents in the Chinese A-share market**. It is not a stock-tip service, and it does not place real brokerage orders.

The intended journey is straightforward: select or connect an agent; configure an experiment; run it against historical information; inspect decisions, orders and account changes; then modify the configuration and run a comparable experiment.

The word **training** here refers to this process of configuration, experimentation and review. It does **not** mean that the system automatically fine-tunes model weights.

### [Slide · Three connected workspaces | ~0:50]

I organise the user experience into three connected workspaces.

In the **Agent Studio**, users choose the agent, its role, tools, model and risk preferences. A configuration version should identify exactly what is being tested.

In the **Arena**, users see the decision stream alongside a simulated portfolio. The goal is to connect what the agent says with what happens to an order, the account, and the portfolio.

In **Review**, users examine the sequence of decisions, portfolio outcomes and candidate differences between runs.

These workspaces are not three unrelated dashboards. Together, they form a feedback loop: **configure, observe, review, and revise**.

### [Slide · Architecture and responsibility boundaries | ~1:00]

The proposed architecture separates the agent from the market simulator.

An **agent adapter** is responsible for connecting to different agent runtimes. In the design, the Agent Client Protocol, or ACP, is a candidate common control interface. A separate tool layer, based on MCP, would expose market observations, historical evidence and trading actions through structured calls.

The simulator—not the language model—must be authoritative for the simulated clock, rule checks, execution outcome and account balance. This matters because an agent saying “I bought 100 shares” is not proof that a legal, filled order exists.

A run also needs an event trail: what the agent observed, what evidence it used, what action it proposed, whether a person intervened, whether the order was accepted or rejected, and how the portfolio changed.

This is the **target backend design**, rather than a statement that the full protocol integration and engine are already deployed.

### [Slide · Three design decisions | ~1:10]

Three decisions are especially important.

**First, point-in-time information.** In a historical experiment, the agent should only access information available at that simulated date. Otherwise, future announcements or revised financial statements can leak into the past, making the result misleading. The proposed gateway therefore checks evidence availability against the simulation clock.

**Second, A-share market rules.** The engine must understand constraints such as T+1 selling, trading suspensions, price limits, lot sizes, available cash and transaction costs. A proposed trade and an executed trade are different events.

**Third, intervention and reproducibility.** If a user changes an order, the record must identify it as human-assisted. If the user re-runs a modified agent, the data window, initial capital and other conditions should remain comparable. A replay of a recorded trace should also be distinguished from a fresh model inference.

These decisions are not decorative features. They protect the meaning of an evaluation result.

The design is ambitious. So the most important question for this Term 1 presentation is: **which pieces are already working, and which are still engineering goals?** Let me separate them clearly.

## 04 · Implementation, Demonstration and Evaluation (~8:00, including ~3:00 demo)

### [Slide · Term 1 implementation scope | ~0:55]

For Term 1, my principal deliverable is a **working, interactive web prototype** of the openOutcry experience. It is implemented with React, TypeScript, Vite and React Router. I focused on making the complete user-facing decision workflow understandable and interactive before connecting a production simulation backend.

The code includes views for agent selection, training tasks, a run workspace, account information, portfolio charts, decision events and review-oriented dialogs. Rather than treating each page as an isolated mock-up, I use a central state machine so that user actions change the visible run state consistently.

I want to emphasise an important boundary: the current prototype uses **fictional sample companies, seeded events and simulated portfolio figures**. The displayed returns are demonstration data—not measured A-share investment performance, not a verified backtest, and not results from a live external agent.

### [Slide · Engineering detail: state and trace | ~1:05]

One implementation choice I would like to highlight is the way an order progresses through states.

A proposed order is only an intention. When the user approves or modifies it, it becomes a submitted order; the account should not change merely because approval occurred. A separate simulated matching action then changes holdings and cash and appends an event. A rejection, by contrast, leaves the account unchanged.

In the prototype, these transitions are implemented through a reducer-based run state machine. The same underlying state feeds the event feed, positions and portfolio statistics, rather than letting different screens maintain contradictory copies.

This is an important **interaction and domain-model prototype**, but the simulated matching price and fee are still hard-coded in the current implementation. Replacing those demo rules with a data-backed, tested execution engine is future work.

### [Slide · What this demonstrates, and what it does not | ~1:00]

The demonstration establishes three useful things about the prototype.

First, a multi-stage decision can be expressed as an understandable sequence rather than a single chat answer. Second, human approval and rejection can be made explicit. Third, the interface can connect order status to a changing portfolio and event history.

It does **not** establish that an external agent is already making decisions autonomously, that the full A-share rule engine is operational, or that results are point-in-time safe. Those need backend integration and dedicated verification.

This distinction matters academically: a coherent interface is progress, but it is not the same thing as a validated experimental platform.

### [Slide · Verification and remaining gaps | ~1:45]

At the software level, the repository defines checks for TypeScript, production builds, unit tests and UI audits. It also includes tests around utility calculations and derived state. These give a route to check code quality and interaction consistency. However, I will not claim a particular pass count or measured performance result without a fresh test run and recorded output.

For the next stage of evaluation, I propose a layered test plan.

**Layer one: interface and state transitions.** Verify approval, modification, rejection, simulated fill, task switching, error states and account invariants. A rejected order, for example, must not alter available cash.

**Layer two: market correctness.** Use a fixed historical dataset and test T+1 restrictions, suspensions, price-limit conditions, fees, position changes and portfolio arithmetic against known expected results.

**Layer three: time integrity.** Introduce an announcement published after the simulated date and verify that the agent cannot retrieve it. This checks whether point-in-time filtering actually works, rather than merely being described in the architecture.

**Layer four: controlled comparisons.** Repeat runs with identical data and rules, record configuration versions and interventions, and distinguish deterministic trace replay from fresh stochastic model decisions. Only then would return, drawdown and comparative charts have defensible experimental meaning.

At the current stage, these latter tests are an **evaluation plan**, not completed empirical findings. That is the central limitation of this Term 1 deliverable.

## Closing · Conclusions and Future Work (~1:30)

### [Slide · Conclusions | ~0:45]

To conclude, openOutcry starts from a shift in the question we ask about financial AI. Instead of asking whether an agent can produce a convincing recommendation, I ask whether users can **evaluate its repeated decisions, understand their consequences, and improve it under comparable conditions**.

This term, I defined the product requirements, investigated relevant agent interfaces and simulation concerns, designed the target architecture, and implemented an interactive frontend prototype that demonstrates the intended user workflow and the separation between a trading proposal, approval and simulated execution.

That is a foundation for an evaluation environment, not yet a completed evaluation environment.

### [Slide · Term 2 priorities | ~0:35]

My next priorities are to connect an agent through the selected protocol, implement a historical A-share data and simulation backend, enforce time-valid evidence access, persist complete event trails, and then run reproducible experiments with explicit risk and performance metrics.

The key milestone is not merely a more polished interface. It is being able to show **one complete, verifiable run**, and then a controlled re-run whose differences can be explained.

### [Slide · Thank you | ~0:10]

Ultimately, openOutcry is about making investment agents **testable before they become trustworthy**. Thank you for your attention. I welcome your questions.
